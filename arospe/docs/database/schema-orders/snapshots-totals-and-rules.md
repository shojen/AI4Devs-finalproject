The other parts are listed in the [hub](../schema-orders.md#table-of-contents).

### The price snapshot — read from the variant, never the parent, when one is named

The story's central invariant, and the one every downstream Orders story (refunds, tax) depends on: `order_items.unit_price` / `.product_sku` / `.product_name` are written **once**, at creation, from whatever the catalog says *right now* — never re-derived, never re-read on render. A later change to `products.price` or `product_variants.price`, or the deletion of either row (`nullOnDelete()` above), never moves an existing line item's snapshot. This is the identical rule [`sales_regions.rate`](../schema-products/sales-regions-and-media.md#sales_regions) and this file's own [address snapshot](#the-address-snapshot) already establish for a different column: never re-derive historical financial (or, there, geographic) data from a mutable source.

**D-15 — a variant line item snapshots its price and SKU from the *variant* row, never the parent product.** `product_variants.price` is `NOT NULL` and fully independent of `products.price` — that is what a variant *is* — so the rule is stated per column, exactly as [`CreateOrder`](../../../app/Actions/Orders/CreateOrder.php) implements it:

- `unit_price` ← `product_variants.price` when a variant is named, else `products.price`.
- `product_sku` ← `product_variants.sku` when a variant is named, else `products.sku` (also why `order_items.product_sku` is capped at the longer of the two lengths).
- `product_name` ← always `products.name`, whether or not a variant is named.

This needed its own decision and its own regression test rather than "obviously the variant" — the *wrong* implementation (resolve the product, read `$product->price`, attach the variant id only as a reference) is the natural one to write, and it passes every test whose fixtures happen to give the product and its variant the same price. `tests/Feature/Orders/` pins the regression by mutating the **variant's** own row and re-fetching the order — a test that mutates only the parent product's price cannot detect this failure mode at all.

#### F-1 (Phase 4 security audit) — resolving a related pair of ids: validate the relationship, not two existences

A line item payload carries **two** ids that must belong together — `product_id` and `product_variant_id` — and validating that each individually exists (`Rule::exists('products', 'id')`, `Rule::exists('product_variants', 'id')`) does not prove the two rows are *related*. The Phase 4 security audit on this story found exactly that gap: `CreateOrder`'s first draft resolved the product and the variant as two independent lookups, so a caller holding only `orders.create` could submit an expensive product's id together with an unrelated, cheap product's variant id and have the line item written with the expensive product's **name** at the cheap variant's **price** — real, direct price manipulation, invisible to every check a reviewer would naturally run (both ids exist, both rows are re-read from the database, every value is copied from a real catalog row).

The shipped fix resolves the variant **through** its own item's already-resolved product, in memory, after both collections are bulk-fetched (never one query per item — a separate finding, **F-2**, on the same method): a variant whose `product_id` does not match the item's own resolved product is refused with the identical `ModelNotFoundException` a nonexistent variant would produce, never silently accepted. See [security/related-id-pair-resolution.md](../../security/related-id-pair-resolution.md) for the full write-up — the vulnerable shape, why the simplest per-item `$product->variants()->findOrFail()` fix could not be used directly here (it would reintroduce the N+1 the bulk-fetch requirement exists to close), and the two other places in this codebase already checked and found safe against the same failure mode.

### Totals are derived and re-derived, not write-once

Story 0045 wrote `subtotal`/`tax_amount`/`total` once, at creation, from the submitted line items — which reads as write-once until a second writer exists to contradict it. Story 0048's [`App\Actions\Orders\RecalculateOrderTotals`](../../../app/Actions/Orders/RecalculateOrderTotals.php) is that second writer: every successful `AddOrderItem` / `RemoveOrderItem` / `UpdateOrderItemQuantity` call recomputes all three columns from scratch, inside the same transaction as the line-item write, by summing `order_items.line_total` fresh rather than incrementally adjusting a stored value. `subtotal += $newLine` was considered and rejected for the same reason story 0048's own D-7 states it: an incremental adjustment drifts the moment any single write is missed or repeated, and the row count on one order is small enough that recomputing from scratch costs nothing worth trading correctness for.

Two invariants this re-derivation preserves, neither obvious from the column list alone:

- **`tax_amount` is `subtotal × (tax_rate ÷ 100)`, computed in exactly one place** — [`App\Actions\Orders\CalculateTaxAmount`](../../../app/Actions/Orders/CalculateTaxAmount.php), shared by `RecalculateOrderTotals` and story 0053's `ResolveOrderTaxRegion` (story 0053a). `tax_rate` is a **percentage** (`21.000` means 21%), rounded half-up to two decimals; 0048 originally shipped without the `÷ 100`, which would have produced a tax 100× too large on the first edit of a resolved order.
- **`tax_amount` recomputes only when `tax_rate` is already non-null** (D-8, unchanged from 0045) — a `NULL` `tax_rate` still means "not configured" after an edit, exactly as it does at creation, so `tax_amount` stays `0.00` and no sales region is resolved as a side effect of a line-item edit. `shipping_amount` is read and summed into `total`, never itself recomputed — selecting or pricing a shipping rate is stories 0037/0054's job, not this recomputation's.
- **`order_items.unit_price` is immutable after insert.** `UpdateOrderItemQuantity` re-multiplies the *existing* `unit_price` column by the new quantity (`line_total = unit_price × quantity`) and never re-reads `$item->product->price` — the story's own highest-risk line (D-4/R-1), proven by a dedicated regression test that mutates the product's catalog price *after* the line item exists, then changes only the quantity, and asserts `unit_price` is unmoved. `AddOrderItem` is the only writer that ever sets `unit_price`, and only once, at the moment a line item is created — matching [the price snapshot](#the-price-snapshot--read-from-the-variant-never-the-parent-when-one-is-named) rule above exactly, now confirmed by a second story rather than merely stated by the first.

Both `AssertWithinColumnCeiling` (the shared `decimal(10,2)` overflow guard `CreateOrder` already applies at creation) and the recomputation itself run inside the same `DB::transaction()` as the line-item write, so a rejected edit — the hard block, a validation failure, a column-overflow refusal — never leaves the stored totals out of sync with the rows they describe.

### The address snapshot

**D-4.** `orders` carries its own frozen copy of the customer's shipping and billing addresses — twelve columns, written once from the resolved `Customer` at creation and never re-read from it afterward. Two reasons: the same "never re-derive historical data from a mutable source" rule the price snapshot follows, and a forward one — PRD §3.2's tax resolution (stories 0053/0054) reads "the **order's** shipping address" and "the **billing** address", and if those columns did not exist here, "the order's address" could only mean a live join to `customers` — so a customer moving from one Sales Region to another would silently change the tax basis of every past order. An order for a customer holding no addresses at all (a legitimate state, per [`customers`](../schema-other/customers.md#customers)) persists twelve `null`s rather than failing.

### `order_number` generation

**D-1.** A UUIDv7 is unreadable over the phone and unwriteable on a delivery note, so `orders` carries a second, human-readable identifier. Format: `ORD-{YYYY}-{NNNNNN}`, a per-year sequence zero-padded to six digits (`ORD-2026-000001`), computed from a count of existing rows already carrying that year's prefix — comfortably inside `VARCHAR(20)`. This count-based generator is the minimum collision protection, not proof of concurrency safety (**R-1**): two administrators creating an order in the same instant can race it, so the UNIQUE index has the last word — `CreateOrder` catches the resulting `23000`/`1062` `QueryException` and retries the whole transaction, up to five attempts, with a freshly recomputed number each time, rather than ever surfacing the collision. `order_number` is never mass-assignable and never caller-supplied; it is derived and written with `forceFill()` from the action alone.

### Delete behaviour — the same three-way rule stated once

`orders` and `order_items` are the first pair of tables in this codebase to exercise all three of this project's foreign-key delete behaviours **at once**, which is what makes the rule behind them statable as one rule rather than left to be inferred from three separate examples scattered across the schema. See [migrations.md](../migrations/delete-behaviour-and-vendored.md#the-three-way-delete-behaviour-rule-cascade-restrict-or-null) for the full statement and every table on either side of it.

`refunds` adds two more **restrict** instances (story 0051, OQ-1 settled): `order_item_id` refuses to let a line item that already carries refund records be deleted — a hard database invariant rather than a rule a future line-item-removal story would have to remember, matching `sales_regions.parent_id`'s "restrict where a cascade would destroy configured/financial data" precedent — and `refunded_by` restricts for the identical "insurance against a soft-deleted parent" reason `orders.customer_id` already establishes.

### Nothing is resolved that this story does not own

A newly created order's `sales_region_id`, `shipping_rate_id` and `tax_rate` are all `null` (never `0`/`0.000` — see above); `flagged_for_review` is `false`; `tax_amount` and `shipping_amount` are `0.00`; and `total` equals `subtotal`. Every one of these is asserted by a dedicated test rather than left to inspection, because a populated column here would look exactly like a working feature while silently masking stories 0037/0053/0054's actual, not-yet-built work.

### Authorization

Gated by [`App\Policies\OrderPolicy`](../../../app/Policies/OrderPolicy.php) — see [architecture/authorization.md](../../architecture/authorization/policies-customers-orders.md#orderpolicy--the-twelfth-policy) for the full roster entry. `orders.*` are already-seeded module permissions (`RolePermissionSeeder::MODULES` has carried `orders` since task 0002) — no catalog change, no new permission, no re-seed. `CreateOrder` self-authorizes `create` as its own first statement, through `App\Actions\Auth\LogRefusedPrivilegedAttempt` rather than a bare `Gate::authorize()`, since this story ships no route and no Livewire component — the action is the only reachable enforcement point that exists today.

Story 0048's `AddOrderItem`/`RemoveOrderItem`/`UpdateOrderItemQuantity` self-authorize `update` on the `Order` the identical way, and each layers a **second**, state-based refusal (`App\Exceptions\OrderNotEditableException`, 409) strictly after the permission check when `status` is `Shipped`/`Delivered` — see [architecture/authorization.md](../../architecture/authorization/domain-invariants.md#order-editability--the-second-state-based-refusal-and-the-shape-three-more-stories-copy) for the full pattern and why it is a direct throw rather than a `Gate`/`OrderPolicy` method.

Story 0051's `App\Actions\Orders\RecordRefund` gates on a **bare** `Gate::authorize('orders.refund')` — a non-CRUD permission (`RolePermissionSeeder::ORDER_PERMISSIONS`), not an `OrderPolicy` ability of its own. Its own second, state-based refusal (payment status must be `Paid`/`PartiallyRefunded`) is a `ValidationException`, not a policy method or a domain exception — see [architecture/authorization.md](../../architecture/authorization/policies-customers-orders.md#orderpolicy--the-twelfth-policy) for why that refusal is deliberately kept out of `OrderPolicy`.

Story 0050's `App\Actions\Orders\CancelOrder` **does** gate through an `OrderPolicy` ability — `cancel`, the policy's **sixth** and this app's first requiring TWO permissions (`orders.edit` **and** `orders.refund`, D-6). `App\Models\Order::isManuallyCancellable()` (reading both `status` and `payment_status`) is the state clause both `OrderPolicy::cancel()`'s hint and `CancelOrder`'s own `OrderCancellationBlockedException` direct-throw wrap — see [architecture/authorization.md](../../architecture/authorization/domain-invariants.md#manual-order-cancellation--the-third-state-based-refusal-and-the-first-ability-requiring-two-permissions) for the full mechanism, including why an ordinary actor's blocked-state refusal (403, via the Gate) differs from a Super Admin's (409, via the direct throw).
